Worked example: graph and ontology instead of narratives

"What fixed this before?" is a relationship question. Answering it with twenty write-ups makes the model re-derive, from prose, a fact you could have stored as a row.

The twenty narratives are near-duplicates. The same failure recurs, so most of those 12,000 tokens restate one event in different words.

The ontology

An ontology is the typed vocabulary of your domain: what kinds of thing exist, and which relationships are allowed. For a simulation platform it is small.

flowchart LR FM["FailureMode"] -->|occurs_in| SV["Solver
+ Version"] FM -->|fixed_by| REM["Remediation"] REM -->|adjusts| PAR["Parameter"] REM -->|results_in| OUT["Outcome"] SK["Skill"] -->|covers| FM FM -->|similar_to| FM

Seven entity kinds, six relations. Three things follow, each saving tokens for a different reason.

Property What it removes
Normalisation Fourteen phrasings of "AMG diverged" collapse to one node. You stop retrieving the same fact fourteen times.
Typed relations You query fixed_by edges instead of hoping the right narratives rank highly.
Aggregation in SQL The database counts wins. The model receives the conclusion, not the evidence for it. 
SELECT r.kind, r.adjusts_parameter,
       count(*) FILTER (WHERE o.succeeded) AS wins,
       count(*)                            AS tries
FROM   mem_nodes f
JOIN   mem_edges e ON e.src_node_id = f.node_id AND e.relation = 'fixed_by'
JOIN   remediations r ON r.node_id = e.dst_node_id
JOIN   outcomes    o ON o.remediation_id = r.id
WHERE  f.node_id = $1 AND f.tenant_id = $2 AND r.solver_version = $3
GROUP  BY r.kind, r.adjusts_parameter
ORDER  BY wins DESC LIMIT 10;
kind          | parameter        | wins | tries | confidence
adjust_field  | courant_number   |   11 |    14 | 0.86
switch_skill  | coupled_pseudo   |    4 |     5 | 0.71
rerun_upstream| mesh.element_size|    2 |     9 | 0.24

About 150 tokens against 12,000, and it carries something the prose did not: success rates. A 0.24 on re-meshing is a fact no amount of reading twenty stories reliably produces.

Modelled, not measured. The prior-fixes row is the 80x improvement; the log row saves the most absolute tokens.

Roughly $0.11 against $0.016 per call at mid-tier input pricing. The multiplier is what sits outside the table: a diagnosis loop runs up to four iterations, and a busy month is thousands of diagnoses.

The ontology is the cost, not the graph

Symptom: a graph project stalls after six months, half-populated and distrusted.

Cause: the ontology was designed to be complete rather than to answer specific questions, so every new source needs a modelling debate.

Mitigation: derive it backwards from the two or three questions you actually ask. Seven entity kinds answered the question above. Write edges as a side effect of work already happening. See Graph RAG.

Store the conclusion, not the evidence

The largest block in the naive prompt was a log that is ~95% near-identical numeric rows.
flowchart LR L["84,000-line log"] --> H["header"] L --> E["error windows"] L --> R["60,000 residual rows"] L --> T["tail"] R --> SUM["one summary chunk
'residuals rose over 23
iterations, diverging from 1e-3'"] H --> IDX[("index")] E --> IDX SUM --> IDX T --> IDX

No query is ever answered by finding one row of a residual table. Indexing them buys nothing and balloons the chunk count, where every chunk matches any residual query equally well.

Written once at ingest, read on every retrieval. The cost amortises to nothing. See Chunking In Depth.

Two assembly habits

Cheap to adopt, awkward to retrofit.
flowchart TD subgraph W["Wrong order"] W1["retrieved chunk"] --> W2["instructions"] --> W3["tool schemas"] W3 --> W4["cache hit rate: 0%"] end subgraph R["Ordered by volatility"] R1["instructions"] --> R2["tool schemas"] --> R3["resources"] --> R4["retrieved chunk"] R4 --> R5["everything above cached"] end

Prefix caching keys on an exact prefix. One retrieved chunk placed high invalidates everything after it.

Progressive disclosure is the other. Fifty playbooks pasted in is ~75,000 tokens per call, ~2% of it relevant. A ~100-token descriptor each, plus one body on a trigger, is ~5,000 + ~1,500. See Agent Skills.

Prefix caching has a minimum length, and small models often need more than their prompts reach. A grader prompt of a few hundred tokens gets nothing. Cache the grader's verdicts instead, keyed by query hash, evidence ID and model.

Call-level levers

Smaller individually, but they apply to every call.
flowchart LR subgraph A["8 calls"] A1["prompt + cand 1"] A2["prompt + cand 2"] A3["… ×8"] end subgraph B["1 call"] B1["prompt + all 8 candidates"] end A -.->|"drops 7 prompt copies
32 round trips → 4"| B
Lever Rule
Route by job Volume work to the small model. Planning is 1-2 calls; grading is up to 32.
Batch inside loops One call over N candidates sends the instructions once.
Cap output, make it typed A schema-bound object cannot pad itself with an explanation nobody reads.
Thinking budget where volume is low Reasoning bills as output and is never cached.
Track a seen-set A loop re-finding the same chunks pays for them every iteration.
Project fields Sending a whole row for three columns is the structured version of the same mistake.

Takeaways

  • Decide what never enters the context before optimising what does.
  • A graph plus a small ontology turns "read twenty write-ups and infer" into ten rows: ~12,000 tokens down to ~150, with success rates the prose never had.
  • Derive the ontology backwards from the questions you ask.
  • Store the conclusion, not the repetitive evidence.
  • Order by volatility, and load playbook bodies only on a trigger.
  • Watch cost per successful outcome, or you optimise into a worse system.
