Tokenomics

Architectural ways to cut token cost, starting with what you never retrieve

The cheapest token is the one you never send

Most token-cost advice starts at the call: shorter prompts, compression, caching. The large savings are upstream, in deciding what enters the context at all.

A RAG pipeline over simulation data makes this concrete. The naive shape is to answer a question by retrieving generously and letting the model sort it out: the last few thousand lines of a solver log, the top twenty documentation chunks, and twenty past-run write-ups. That is a defensible prototype and an indefensible production bill, because almost all of it is repetition the model must read past.

The levers below are ordered by how much they save, which is roughly the reverse of how often they get discussed.

The worked example: graph and ontology instead of narrative retrieval

"What fixed something like this before?" is a relationship question. Answering it by retrieving twenty past-run write-ups makes the model re-derive, from prose, a fact you could have stored as a row.

What the naive pipeline sends

A simulation step fails. Without a graph, every source is a pile of text, and breadth is the only way to be confident:

Block What it is Input tokens
System prompt and rules Instructions, tool schemas ~800
Solver log tail Last ~2,000 lines, mostly residual tables ~26,000
Documentation chunks Top 20 at ~800 tokens ~16,000
Past-run write-ups Top 20 narratives at ~600 tokens ~12,000
Total per diagnosis call — ~54,800
Modelled, not measured: a plausible "retrieve generously" baseline for one diagnosis.

Two things are wrong beyond the size. The twenty past-run narratives are near-duplicates of each other, because the same failure family recurs, so most of those 12,000 tokens restate the same event in different words. And the model has to infer the answer from them: read twenty stories, notice that lowering the Courant number appears in fourteen of them, notice that eleven of those ended green, and conclude. That inference is exactly the work a database does for free.

What the ontology changes

An ontology is the typed vocabulary of your domain: the entity kinds that exist, and the relationships allowed between them. For a simulation platform it is small.

Entities            Relationships
---------           -------------------------------------
Solver              FailureMode  --occurs_in-->  Solver
SolverVersion       FailureMode  --fixed_by-->   Remediation
FailureMode         Remediation  --adjusts-->    Parameter
Parameter           Remediation  --results_in--> Outcome
Remediation         Skill        --covers-->     FailureMode
Outcome             FailureMode  --similar_to--> FailureMode
Skill

Three things follow from having it, and each one saves tokens for a different reason.

Normalisation kills the duplicates. Fourteen write-ups describing "AMG solver diverged" in fourteen phrasings collapse to one canonical FailureMode node. You stop paying to retrieve the same fact fourteen times.

Typed relations let you query instead of search. "What fixed this" becomes a walk along fixed_by edges filtered by solver and version, rather than a similarity search hoping the right narratives rank highly.

Aggregation happens in SQL, not in the context window. The database counts the successes and computes the rates. The model receives the conclusion, not the evidence for it.

-- Two-hop walk, filtered by tenant and version, aggregated in the database
SELECT r.kind, r.adjusts_parameter,
       count(*) FILTER (WHERE o.succeeded) AS wins,
       count(*)                            AS tries,
       round(avg(e.weight)::numeric, 2)    AS confidence
FROM   mem_nodes f
JOIN   mem_edges e ON e.src_node_id = f.node_id AND e.relation = 'fixed_by'
JOIN   remediations r ON r.node_id = e.dst_node_id
JOIN   outcomes    o ON o.remediation_id = r.id
WHERE  f.node_id = $1            -- canonical FailureMode, found by one vector lookup
  AND  f.tenant_id = $2
  AND  r.solver_version = $3
GROUP  BY r.kind, r.adjusts_parameter
ORDER  BY wins DESC
LIMIT  10;

What comes back is ten rows, and they go into the prompt as ten rows:

kind          | parameter        | wins | tries | confidence
adjust_field  | courant_number   |   11 |    14 | 0.86
switch_skill  | coupled_pseudo   |    4 |     5 | 0.71
rerun_upstream| mesh.element_size|    2 |     9 | 0.24

Roughly 150 tokens, against ~12,000 for the narratives, and it carries information the narratives did not: success rates. A 0.24 confidence on re-meshing is a fact no amount of reading twenty stories reliably produces.

Modelled comparison for one diagnosis. The prior-fixes row is the 80x improvement; the log row is the one that saves the most absolute tokens.

At mid-tier input pricing that is roughly $0.11 against $0.016 per call. The multiplier that matters is what sits outside this table: a diagnosis loop runs up to four iterations, so the naive shape is paying fifty-thousand-token prompts four times over, and a busy month is thousands of diagnoses.

The graph is not free, and the ontology is the cost

Symptom: a graph project stalls after six months with a half-populated schema nobody trusts.

Cause: the ontology was designed to be complete rather than to answer specific questions, so every new source needs a modelling debate before it can be ingested.

Mitigation: derive the ontology backwards from the two or three questions you actually ask. Seven entity kinds and six relations answered the question above. Add an entity kind only when a real query needs it, and write edges as a side effect of work already happening rather than as a separate curation project. See Graph RAG.

Summarise repetition at ingest

The largest single block in the naive prompt was a solver log that is ~95% near-identical numeric rows.

An 84,000-line solver log is mostly iteration-by-iteration residual tables. No query is ever answered by finding one row of a residual table, so indexing them buys nothing and costs a ballooned chunk count where every chunk matches any residual query equally well.

Computing one summary at ingest replaces them: "residuals rose over 23 consecutive iterations, diverging from 1e-3". That sentence is the fact the 60,000 rows were evidence for. It is written once and read on every retrieval, so the cost amortises to nothing.

The general rule: when a source is repetitive, store the conclusion rather than the evidence, and keep a reference to the raw data for the rare case somebody needs it. See Chunking In Depth.

Progressive disclosure and prefix ordering

Two assembly habits that cost nothing to adopt and are hard to retrofit.

Progressive disclosure. Fifty operational playbooks pasted into a system prompt is ~75,000 tokens on every call, roughly 2% of it relevant to the failure actually present. Splitting each into a ~100-token descriptor that stays resident and a body that loads only when its trigger matches turns that into ~5,000 tokens, plus ~1,500 when one fires. The registry is what you pay for; the bodies are free until read. See Agent Skills.

Ordering by volatility. Prefix caching keys on an exact byte-for-byte prefix, so a single retrieved chunk placed above your stable instructions invalidates everything after it. Put instructions, tool schemas and resources first, retrieved evidence last.

Prefix caching has a minimum cacheable length, and small models often require more than their prompts reach. A grader prompt of a few hundred tokens gets no benefit at all. Cache the grader's verdicts instead, keyed by query hash, evidence ID and model, since the verdict is a pure function of those three.

Call-level levers

Smaller wins individually, but they apply to every call and compound.
  • Route by job. Planning is once or twice per run and wants the strongest reasoning. Grading is up to 32 calls per diagnosis against an explicit rubric and does not. Routing the volume work to a small model changes cost per run by roughly an order of magnitude. See Model Routing & Cost Engineering.
  • Batch inside loops. Grading eight candidates one call each repeats the whole instruction block eight times. One batched call sends it once, and cuts 32 sequential round trips to 4.
  • Cap output, and make it typed. Output tokens cost several times input. A schema-bound object with a tight max_tokens cannot pad itself with an explanation nobody reads.
  • Spend thinking budget where volume is low. Reasoning tokens bill as output and are never cached, so adding even 500 per grading call multiplies the dominant cost line. Put the budget on the single final proposal instead. See Extended Thinking.
  • Track a seen-set. A retrieval loop that re-finds the same chunks pays for them every iteration. Deduplicating by evidence ID both terminates the loop earlier and stops the repeat charge.
  • Project fields. Passing a whole row when the task needs three columns is the structured-data version of retrieving twenty narratives.

Measuring it, so a regression is visible

Cost per run belongs on a dashboard next to accuracy, or one of them silently degrades to improve the other.

Return cost as part of each activity's typed result rather than writing it to a side table, so it replays deterministically with the run's history and cannot be double-counted by a retry or lost to a crash. Record reasoning tokens separately from ordinary output, or a thinking-budget change looks like a traffic increase.

Metric Catches
Input tokens per call, by role A prompt that quietly grew, or a funnel stage that stopped narrowing
Cache read ratio Someone reordered the prompt and killed the prefix cache
Cost per run, median and p95 A long tail of runs looping more than anyone expected
Cost per successful outcome Savings that were actually accuracy losses in disguise
The last row is the one that keeps the others honest.

When cost becomes binding, cut in this order: the reranker where the pre-filtered pool is already within ~3x the final top-k, then route more work to the mid-tier model, then lower the loop iteration cap. The first two are invisible to quality. The third shows up in the escalation rate, which is the honest kind of cut: it appears in a number somebody watches.

Takeaways

  • The biggest savings are upstream of the call. Decide what never enters the context before optimising what does.
  • A graph plus a small ontology turns "read twenty write-ups and infer" into ten aggregated rows, roughly 12,000 tokens down to 150, and adds success rates the prose never carried.
  • Derive the ontology backwards from the questions you ask. Seven entity kinds beat a complete model nobody finishes.
  • Store the conclusion, not the repetitive evidence, and keep a reference to the raw data.
  • Progressive disclosure and volatility ordering are cheap to adopt and awkward to retrofit.
  • Watch cost per successful outcome, not cost per call, or you will optimise your way into a worse system.
